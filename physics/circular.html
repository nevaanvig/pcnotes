1 · What the loop demands

ac = v²/r, pointing at the centre
so ΣF towards the centre = mv²/r = N
at θ = 0° from the bottom

Centripetal force is a job, not a new kind of force. Nothing ever appears in a free-body diagram labelled 'centripetal' — you draw the real forces (here the track's normal force and gravity), add up their components towards the centre, and set that total equal to mv²/r.

2 · At the top and at the bottom

top: N + mg = mv²/r → N = N
bottom: N − mg = mv²/r → N = N
general: N = mv²/r + mg cos θ = + = N

At the top, gravity points the same way as the needed force, so the track has less to do. At the bottom it points the opposite way, so the track must supply the centripetal force and hold the weight up. That is why you feel heaviest at the bottom of a loop.

3 · The minimum speed

at the limit the track stops pushing: N = 0
then mg alone must equal mv²/r
vmin = √(gr) = m s⁻¹
—

Set N = 0 at the top and the mass cancels: a heavy car and a light one need exactly the same minimum speed. Below it, the required force is more than gravity can supply at that radius, so the object simply stops following the circle and becomes a projectile.

4 · The 6mg result

Energy conservation gives vbottom² = vtop² + 4gr, and substituting that into the two N equations makes everything cancel except 6mg. Change the speed, the radius, anything — the difference stays 6mg. It is one of the neatest results in the whole topic.

The bob is not in equilibrium — it is accelerating towards the centre of its circle the whole time. But it is not accelerating vertically, so the vertical components must still cancel exactly. Splitting the tension into those two directions is the entire solution.

2 · The geometry

r = L sin θ = × sin ° = m
T = mg / cos θ = N
T / mg = ×

The radius of the circle is not the string length — it is the string length times sin θ. And because cos θ < 1, the tension is always bigger than the weight, and it runs away to infinity as the string approaches horizontal. A perfectly horizontal string is impossible: there would be no vertical component left to hold the bob up.

3 · Dividing one by the other

T sin θ / T cos θ = (mv²/r)/(mg)
tan θ = v²/gr =
v = √(gr tan θ) = m s⁻¹

Dividing the two equations kills both T and m in one move. What is left connects only the angle, the speed and the radius — and it is exactly the same relationship as the banked curve, because both are the same physics: one slanted force doing two jobs at once.

4 · The period

period = 2π√(L cos θ / g) = s
mass m = kg does not appear

L cos θ is the vertical depth of the bob below the pivot, so a conical pendulum keeps time like a simple pendulum of that depth. Swing it wider and faster and the period actually gets shorter, which is the opposite of most people's intuition.

Tilt the road and the normal force tilts with it, so part of it now points towards the centre of the bend. That horizontal slice is what turns the car — no friction required. This is why racetracks and motorway slip roads are banked at all.

2 · The design speed

v = √(gr tan θ) = m s⁻¹
= km h⁻¹  (r = m, θ = °)

Every banked curve has exactly one speed at which it works perfectly with no sideways grip needed. Note the mass has cancelled again — a loaded truck and a motorbike share the same design speed, which is precisely why the engineer can choose one angle for all traffic.

3 · Friction widens the window

slowest: v = √(gr(tan θ − μ)/(1 + μ tan θ)) = m s⁻¹

Above the design speed the car tends to slide up the bank, so friction acts down the slope; below it the car tends to slip down, so friction acts up. Swapping that sign is the only difference between the two formulas. When μ tan θ reaches 1 the denominator vanishes and there is no upper limit at all.

4 · Why it feels different

N = mg / cos θ = N, versus mg = N

On a banked turn the seat pushes on you harder than your own weight, and the extra push is what you feel pressing you into the seat. The graph shows N/mg climbing steeply past about 50° — which is why heavily banked turns are punishing for the driver.

Try this: in the vertical loop, drop the speed at the top until the warning turns red — that threshold is exactly √(gr), where gravity alone supplies the whole centripetal force. The graph makes the rest obvious: N and mv²/r rise and fall together, separated everywhere by mg cos θ, and N at the bottom minus N at the top is always 6mg no matter what you change. In the other two modes you land on the same equation, tan θ = v²/gr, because both resolve one slanted force into a vertical part that balances the weight and a horizontal part that turns the object.
