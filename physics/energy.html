1 · The two mechanical stores

Ek = ½mv² — energy of motion
ΔEp = mgΔh — energy of position in a gravity field
at release: E = mgh = × 9.81 × = J

Potential energy is always a change, measured from wherever you choose to call zero. That choice is free — only differences ever matter — so pick the lowest point in the problem and save yourself the arithmetic.

2 · Conservation, and why mass cancels

mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = m s⁻¹
now: KE + PE + thermal = J

The m on both sides cancels, so the landing speed of a dropped object does not depend on its mass at all — the same result as free fall in A.1.5, arrived at from energy instead of SUVAT. Whichever route you take must give the same answer, which is a useful way to check yourself.

3 · Where the energy goes

lost per bounce = (1 − e²) × the kinetic energy arriving
with e = that is % each time
so the rebound height is e²h = m

The coefficient of restitution e is a speed ratio, but kinetic energy goes as v², so the energy ratio is e². That square is why a ball with e = 0.9 — which sounds almost perfect — still loses nearly a fifth of its energy on every single bounce.

4 · The green bar is the whole point

KE + PE + thermal = constant, always

Energy is never destroyed, only moved. Switch the restitution to 1 and the drag to 0 and the green bar is frozen while the red and blue ones trade places. Add losses and the green bar still does not move — the energy has simply moved into the grey thermal column instead of leaving the universe.

Try this: set e = 1 and drag = 0 first. The green total bar freezes while red and blue trade places perfectly — that is conservation of energy with nothing else going on. Now drop e to 0.85: the total bar still does not move, because the lost energy appears in the grey thermal column rather than vanishing. Watch the rebound height fall as e², so 0.85 gives back only 72% of the height. Finally add air drag and the graph's red and blue curves stop reaching the same peaks at all.
