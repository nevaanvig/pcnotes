1 · Hooke's law

F = kx, with k the spring constant in N m⁻¹
k = N m⁻¹, pulled to x = m
so F = N at full stretch

The force is not constant — it grows in proportion to how far you have already stretched the spring. That single fact is why the energy stored is not simply Fx, and why a graph is the safest way to think about it.

2 · Why the energy has a ½ in it

W = area under the F–x graph
the graph is a straight line from the origin, so the area is a triangle
Ee = ½ × base × height = ½xF = ½kx²
= J at full stretch

Average force is what the ½ really represents: the force climbed evenly from 0 to kx, so on average it was ½kx. Multiply that by the distance x and you get ½kx². Exactly the same reasoning gives the ½ in ½mv² and in s = ½at².

3 · Trading energy back and forth

total = ½kx² + ½mv² = constant
now: elastic J + kinetic J
all elastic at the ends, all kinetic at the middle
vmax = A√(k/m) = m s⁻¹

At maximum extension the block is momentarily stationary, so every joule is elastic. Passing through the natural length the spring is unstretched, so every joule is kinetic. Setting those two equal — ½kA² = ½mv²max — is the quickest route to the maximum speed.

4 · The period

T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(/) = s

Notice the amplitude is nowhere in the formula. Pull the block twice as far and it travels twice as far but moves twice as fast, and the two effects cancel exactly. That independence is what makes oscillators useful as clocks.

Try this: watch the shaded triangle on the F–x graph as the block moves — its area is the elastic energy, and it is a triangle rather than a rectangle precisely because the force grows from zero. The red and blue bars swap completely twice per cycle while the green total stays put. Now change the amplitude and check the period readout: it does not move, because T = 2π√(m/k) contains no A. Add damping and the total bar finally starts to fall.
