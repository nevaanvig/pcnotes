1 · What power actually is

P = W/t — the rate of doing work, in watts
and since W = Fs, P = Fs/t = Fv
F = N at v = m s⁻¹
so P = kW

P = Fv is the version worth memorising, because most questions give you a steady speed rather than a time. It also says something useful about cars: at a fixed engine power, the available force falls as the speed rises, which is why you change down to accelerate or climb.

2 · The force you have to supply

at constant speed the resultant is zero, so driving force = everything resisting
gravity component: mg sin θ = N
drag: cv² = N
total F = N

Constant speed does not mean no force — it means the forces balance. On the flat the engine fights drag alone; on a hill it fights drag plus the component of weight down the slope. Set the angle to zero and the purple arrow disappears entirely.

3 · Why top speed is so expensive

drag grows as v², so the power to beat it grows as v³
doubling the speed multiplies the useful power by ×

This is the single most important consequence of P = Fv. Against drag alone, going twice as fast needs eight times the power — which is why a car that cruises happily at 100 km/h needs an enormous engine for 200, and why cyclists care so much about their riding position.

4 · Efficiency

η = useful output ÷ total input
kW ÷ kW = %
wasted as heat and sound: kW

Efficiency is a ratio, so it has no units and can never exceed 1. A petrol engine manages around 25%, which means three quarters of the fuel's energy leaves as heat before it ever reaches the wheels. The Sankey diagram makes the scale of that waste hard to ignore.

Try this: leave the incline at zero so drag is the only thing to beat, then watch the green curve on the graph. It is not a straight line — it is a cubic, because drag grows as v² and power is that times v again. Double the speed from 15 to 30 and the power needed goes up roughly eightfold. Now raise the incline: the purple line is the gravity contribution and it is straight, since mg sin θ does not change with speed. The Sankey shows how little of the input ever reaches the road at a realistic 25% efficiency.
